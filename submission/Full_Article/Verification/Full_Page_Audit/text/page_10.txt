   For a continuously distributed population, an isolated pair of points with the same
normalized direction illustrates a possible collision, but does not itself prove positive
classification error. An exact point or ray has zero probability under the continuous area
distributions used here. What matters is the class-conditional probability distributed
across intervals of angles. Once distance is discarded, a classifier must assign the same
label to every position represented by a given angle; if competing decision preferences
occur on a positive-measure set of angles, it cannot reproduce the full-coordinate Bayes
rule. The following identity quantifies precisely that loss, by integrating the opposing
class-weighted preferences along radius and then across direction.


Why one ray can force classification error. A classifier that sees the original
coordinates may give different labels to points at different distances along the same
ray. After normalization it sees only the angle and must make one decision for all of
them. To count the probability of the conflicting decisions, define the signed density
d = π0 f0 − π1 f1 : its positive and negative regions favor classes 0 and 1, respectively.
