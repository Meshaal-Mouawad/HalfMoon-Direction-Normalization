midpoint displacement, direction-only features have a positive floor for every origin even
though the original classes can be separated perfectly. At the overlapping displacement,
raw ambiguity and representational ambiguity coexist. These distinctions permit error
to be allocated to its correct cause instead of describing every increase in test error as
a network deficiency.


8.2    Relation to contemporary representation and decision

       research

The fixed-embedding comparisons of Poblete et al. [3] concern which downstream rule
matches existing learned feature geometry. The present all-origin theory concerns
whether a feature transformation preserves the information required by the optimal
decision in the first place. This distinction is consequential: once the direction map
merges opposite class preferences, replacing the learner with a different classifier cannot
eliminate the corresponding population loss.
   Ahmadian and Lindsten [4] provide independent empirical motivation for studying
normalization-sensitive performance under a radial-kernel anomaly-detection objective.
Our result identifies and quantifies an explicit geometric mechanism in a different task,
binary Bayes classification. The experimental scores are not interchangeable, but both
studies show that removing or retaining magnitude must be judged relative to the
decisions the representation is intended to support.
   The broader statistical-risk literature [5–7] establishes decision preservation as a
criterion for useful representations. The present contribution makes that criterion
computable for a moving-origin direction map. The ray identity measures the mass
of opposing decisions lost by normalization, the convex-geometric argument rules out
lossless finite views, and the uniform projection limit rules out sequences of distant
origins approaching zero loss. The lower certificates translate these arguments into a
positive quantitative guarantee for the entire plane. They are intentionally conservative:
